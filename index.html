I am a PhD Candidate in Economics at Victoria University of Wellington, supervised by Ilan Noy. My research examines economic behaviour and policy when adjustment capacity and information are limited. I combine economic theory with empirical analysis to study how households, communities, and public institutions respond to major shocks, how institutional capacity shapes those responses, and how incomplete or selectively observed information affects decision-making, measurement, and regulation.

Shocks, Adaptation, and Institutional Capacity

This research agenda studies how households, communities, and public institutions respond to major shocks, and how differences in adjustment and implementation capacity shape economic behaviour, recovery, and public-sector outcomes. My current work approaches these questions from both household and government perspectives, combining linked administrative data, economic theory, and empirical evidence.

Research Projectwith Ilan Noy · using the New Zealand Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI)

Disaster Resilience and Long-Run Recovery

This research project uses linked New Zealand administrative data to study how major disasters reshape residential mobility, population distribution, and patterns of adjustment over time. Current work focuses on the Christchurch earthquakes, including migration out of and into affected areas, the persistence of relocation, and where people move after leaving Christchurch. The project is designed as a broader research program that can support a series of studies on post-disaster adaptation, resilience, and recovery.

Working Paper

Implementation Capacity under Disaster Stress: Emergency Administrative Behavior and Fiscal Resilience

This paper studies how government implementation capacity shapes administrative and fiscal responses under disaster stress. I develop a sequential model in which implementation capacity is accumulated before a disaster, while emergency administrative shortcuts and recovery liabilities are determined afterward. Using precipitation disasters across Chinese prefectures as external administrative stress tests, the empirical analysis examines whether observable emergency behavior reveals underlying differences in government capacity and fiscal resilience.

Information, Measurement, and Regulation

My research in this area studies how economic decisions and regulatory performance change when information is scarce, selectively observed, or endogenously acquired. The common focus is on how information constraints affect what decision-makers learn, what regulators can measure, and how policy should respond.

Working Paper

Better Credit Review, Less Aggregate Information: Endogenous Review Allocation

This theory paper studies endogenous information acquisition when scarce review capacity is allocated across heterogeneous credit exposures. Better review technology can improve inference under every fixed allocation while inducing review to shift toward exposures that are less informative about the common credit condition, potentially reducing aggregate information even as individual review improves.

Working Paper

Regulating Service Quality Under Selective Observability

This paper studies regulation when public performance measures observe only a selected subset of economically relevant outcomes. Using New York City ride-hailing data, it examines how completed-service performance can improve while performance across submitted requests deteriorates, and develops a framework for deciding when regulators should escalate from routine public monitoring to richer information review.
