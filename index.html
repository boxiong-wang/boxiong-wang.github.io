I am a PhD Candidate in Economics at Victoria University of Wellington, supervised by Ilan Noy. My research studies how economic agents and public institutions respond to shocks when adjustment capacity and information are limited. My work develops along two connected lines: disaster resilience, institutional capacity, and long-run recovery; and information, measurement, and regulation.

Research Projectwith Ilan Noy · Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI)

Christchurch Earthquake Recovery and Residential Reallocation

This project uses linked New Zealand administrative data to study long-run recovery and population reallocation following the Christchurch earthquakes. It examines migration out of and into affected areas, the persistence of relocation, and destination choice, with the broader aim of understanding how major disasters reshape population patterns over time.

Working Paper

Implementation Capacity under Disaster Stress: Emergency Administrative Behavior and Fiscal Resilience

This paper studies how pre-disaster implementation capacity shapes emergency administrative behavior and fiscal resilience under disaster stress. A sequential model shows that visible shortcuts need not reliably reveal underlying capacity, and the empirical analysis uses precipitation disasters across Chinese prefectures as external administrative stress tests.

Information, Measurement, and Regulation

Working Paper

Better Credit Review, Less Aggregate Information: Endogenous Review Allocation

This theory paper studies endogenous information acquisition when scarce review capacity is allocated across heterogeneous credit exposures. Better review technology can improve inference under every fixed allocation while inducing a reallocation of review toward less systematically informative exposures, reducing information about the common credit condition.

Working Paper

Regulating Service Quality Under Selective Observability

This paper studies regulation when public performance measures observe only a selected subset of economically relevant outcomes. Using New York City ride-hailing data, it examines how completed-service performance can improve while performance across submitted requests deteriorates, and develops a framework for deciding when regulators should escalate from routine public monitoring to richer information review.
